On my [Christian Urmi video](/post/christian-urmi/), @jacob_and_william asked in the comments if I could try speaking Aramaic myself, and guessed that it would come out as a hodgepodge of dialects. Which is fair enough, because I picked up my Aramaic from a lot of different places. I was curious which of them would win out when I actually had to produce something, the {{< tooltip term="Gemara" key="gemara" >}}, or {{< tooltip term="Onkelos" key="onkelos" >}}, the Aramaic translation of the Torah.

I read Onkelos every week. There's a practice called *{{< tooltip term="shnayim mikra ve’echad targum" key="shnayim-mikra" >}}*, where you go through the weekly Torah portion twice in the Hebrew and once in the Aramaic translation. Since the Hebrew is easier for me, by the time I get to the Onkelos I already know what the verse is saying, and a lot of the time I'm more or less reciting it. So I've had a lot of steady exposure, but it isn't the Aramaic I've had to work the hardest at.

With the Gemara it's the opposite. It goes back and forth between Hebrew and Aramaic, and I put much more effort into figuring out what it's saying. My guess was that if I had to come up with sentences on the spot, it would sound more like the {{< tooltip term="Bavli" key="talmud-bavli" >}}, the Babylonian Talmud, since that's the Aramaic I've had to wrestle with.

## How I did it

So I took {{< tooltip term="Aesop" key="aesop" >}}'s *North Wind and the Sun* and translated it out loud, a phrase at a time, without looking anything up. I didn't want to be the only judge of how it went, so before I started, I gave ChatGPT a rubric to grade the transcript with. Did I get the story across, did I have the right words, were they in the right forms, did it sound natural, and how much did the grammar sound like the Aramaic I was going for. The first time I had it grade against Onkelos, and then I gave it the same transcript again against the Bavli, to see what would change.

The cold attempt took me less than three minutes, which I know because I timed it. Apparently I didn't record it, though, so the video has me reading back what I came up with at about the same pace as the first attempt. My pronunciation goes back and forth between American {{< tooltip term="yeshiva" key="yeshiva" >}} pronunciation and Israeli Hebrew, and I'm aware of it. Practically all of my mental effort went into coming up with the Aramaic, so once I had a word I said it however it came out and went on.

In the tables, the first column is the English and the second is what I said. After that there are two corrected versions that ChatGPT made, one in {{< tooltip term="Jewish Literary Aramaic" key="jla" >}} (JLA), which is the Aramaic of Onkelos, and one in the Aramaic of the Bavli, and then my notes.

## How much Gemara came out

So how much Gemara came out? Against the Bavli, ChatGPT gave the cold attempt 93 out of 100, and against Onkelos it gave it 85. That's from one pass each on the transcript I typed up from the cold take, so I wouldn't put too much weight on the exact numbers.

Going through my column, I count <span dir="rtl">ההוא גברא</span> four times, plus <span dir="rtl">מינייהו</span>, <span dir="rtl">בתר הכי</span>, <span dir="rtl">טובא</span>, <span dir="rtl">לאלתר</span> and <span dir="rtl">גלימא</span>, which are all Bavli. Then <span dir="rtl">יתחשב תקיף מן חבריה</span> is straight Onkelos, it's word for word the same as the JLA version, and so are <span dir="rtl">ובכן</span> and <span dir="rtl">מצלח</span>. The one place where I couldn't come up with the Aramaic quickly enough was the sunrise, so I just used the Hebrew <span dir="rtl">זרח</span>, where Onkelos has <span dir="rtl">דנח</span>. So there's some hodgepodge in there, like the commenter said, but it's a hodgepodge that's mostly Gemara.

I think the thing that gave me away was *ka*, <span dir="rtl">קא</span>. It's a small word and you don't notice it much when you're learning, but in the Gemara, when somebody is in the middle of doing something, it's *hava ka* and then a verb. Apparently that's how I talk too. The man walking on the road, the man wrapping himself up, the wind blowing, all of it came out with *ka*, seven times in under three minutes, and you won't find that in Onkelos.

My favorite mistake was the last line. I was trying to hold on to “of the two,” so I said <span dir="rtl">דשמשא תקיף מן תרוייהו</span>, which can mean that the sun was stronger than both of them. That's an impressive thing for the sun to pull off, considering it was one of them. When I read it back for the video, I said <span dir="rtl">מינה</span> instead, that the sun was stronger than the wind.
