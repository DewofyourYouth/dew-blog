My father and I learned paragraphs 72–73 of {{< tooltip term="Tzidkat HaTzaddik" key="tzidkat-hatzaddik" >}}. We started with miracles. {{< tooltip term="Rav Tzadok" key="rav-tzadok" >}} says:

> אפשר לעשות מופתים על־פי התורה.
>
> You can work miracles through Torah.[^72]

That was how {{< tooltip term="Moshe" key="moshe-rabbeinu" >}} did his miracles. He opened a new gate in the words of Torah, and through the light of those words there was an influx of light into the world to do whatever it was he was doing.

You can also work miracles through prayer.

<!--more-->

## Why write down the miracles?

If you notice, for most of the prophets, we do not get a whole account of their miracles. Then suddenly you get to {{< tooltip term="Eliyahu" key="eliyahu" def="The prophet Elijah, who challenged the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel and was taken up to heaven in a whirlwind (1 Kings 17–2 Kings 2)." >}} and {{< tooltip term="Elisha" key="elisha" >}}, and there are tons of them.

The Sages tell us:

> נבואה שהוצרכה לדורות נכתבה, ושלא הוצרכה לא נכתבה.
>
> Prophecy needed for future generations was written down; what was not needed was not written down.

There were lots of prophets and lots of things they said. God telling you something does not necessarily mean He wants you to write it down. He might want you to deliver the message, understand it, whatever. So if these stories were written down, they must be needed {{< tooltip term="לדורות" key="ledorot" def="“For the generations”: for all future generations." >}}. Even though the miracles themselves were only for that time, Rav Tzadok says they opened gates.

My father asked whether I meant prophecy in general.

The Torah of Moshe is still around. We understand why we need it. Otherwise, how would we know what to do? It is the basis of all of Judaism. But these miracles happened, did whatever they needed to do, and are no longer happening. Why do we need all these accounts of them?

I could come up with answers. The question did not seem like a very strong question to me. But fine, let's read what he says.

## Eliyahu and Elisha

All of Eliyahu's miracles were through Torah. Rav Tzadok gives the example of the fire coming down on Mount Carmel: it came through a {{< tooltip term="חידוש תורה" key="chiddush" >}} permitting the {{< tooltip term="bamah" key="bamah" >}}, the altar outside the Temple.

This is the famous contest between Eliyahu and the prophets of Baal. You can do whatever you want, but you cannot light the fire yourself. Baal did not manage to get anything going. Eliyahu had them soak the altar with water. Why not? Soak the whole thing. Then the fire came down and everything caught fire.[^carmel]

So how was he able to do that? Rav Tzadok says he had a chiddush in Torah that allowed him to make this altar outside the Temple. Through that chiddush, he was able to work miracles.

The altar needed permission because the Temple was standing. Once the Temple was built, offering on a bamah anywhere else was forbidden. The {{< tooltip term="Gemara" key="gemara" >}} treats Carmel as a one-time ruling for the emergency, on the authority of a prophet.[^bamah] A lot of what Eliyahu did looks like that: strange things done on the authority of prophecy. Rav Tzadok does not call it a prophetic suspension of the law, though. He calls it a chiddush in Torah. The miracle came out of the Torah, not around it.

Elisha's miracles were through prayer. Obviously, Eliyahu also prayed. But the miracles of Eliyahu came through a chiddush in Torah, opening a gate through which the light came. Rav Tzadok brings the Gemara's phrase for him: ואלישע דעביד ברחמי, Elisha, who worked through supplication.

And Elisha merited a double portion, twice as much as Eliyahu, even though he was Eliyahu's student:[^pi-shnayim]

> כי יותר יש לפעול על־ידי תפלה. אבל מכל מקום מה שעל־פי תורה גדול.
>
> More can be accomplished through prayer. Nevertheless, what comes through Torah is greater.

You can do more through prayer, but Torah is on a higher level.

He brings the Gemara in {{< tooltip term="Shabbos" key="shabbos-tractate" def="The tractate of the Talmud on the laws of the Sabbath." >}}:

> מניחים חיי עולם ועוסקים בחיי שעה.
>
> They leave eternal life and occupy themselves with the life of the moment.

I explained it as the complaint about spending too long praying when you could be learning.[^shabbat] Usually, when you pray, you are praying for things. Even the most lofty prayers are for the redemption, for {{< tooltip term="Mashiach" key="mashiach" >}} to come. Torah is eternal. So even though you can accomplish more through prayer, Torah is greater.

## והמעמיק יבין

We spent a while trying to work out what Rav Tzadok was building toward with his two kinds of miracles, and why the stories are needed לדורות. We didn't really come up with anything, and as far as I can find, he never comes back to it later in the book.[^maamik]

Then we noticed how he ends the paragraph: {{< tooltip term="והמעמיק יבין" key="vehamaamik" def="“One who goes deep will understand”: a signal that more lies beneath the stated point." >}}, “one who goes deep will understand.”

Clearly, we are not המעמיק.

{{< admonition type="note" title="Reading it again afterward" open=true >}}
I think at least the לדורות question answers itself in the paragraph, in the words הם פתחו שערים בזה, “they opened gates in this.” The miracles were for their moment; the fire on Carmel is not still burning. What lasts is the gate. The next word is כי, and it says which gates: Eliyahu's miracles all came through Torah, and Elisha's all came through prayer. Each one opened a channel that stays open, and from then on both paths are there for every generation. That is why the stories had to be written down. It is the same language he used for Moshe at the start, who פתח שער חדש בדברי תורה, opened a new gate in the words of Torah. So the two kinds of miracles are not a separate point from לדורות. They are the explanation of it.
{{< /admonition >}}

## Same woman, same desire

Then we went on to {{< tooltip term="ע״ג" key="otiyot" def="Paragraph 73." >}}:

> כשהאדם מתקן מה שחטא, אז ה׳ יתברך מזמין לו אותו פרק ואותו מקום, כדי שתהיה תשובת המשקל.
>
> When a person repairs the sin he committed, {{< tooltip term="Hashem" key="hashem" >}} brings him to the same time and place, so that there will be {{< tooltip term="תשובת המשקל" key="teshuvat-hamishkal" >}}.[^73]

I brought the {{< tooltip term="Rambam" key="rambam" >}}'s description of complete repentance. You have the same opportunity, with the same woman and the same desire, and you hold back. You don't do it. That is complete {{< tooltip term="teshuva" key="teshuvah" >}}.[^rambam]

Obviously, you are not allowed to seek this out yourself. If you did something wrong with a woman, you cannot decide that, in order to do teshuva, you are going to put yourself back in the exact situation where you screwed up.

What could possibly go wrong?

Rav Tzadok points out that you would already be violating the {{< tooltip term="prohibition on seclusion" key="yichud" >}}. Even if you succeed in resisting the sin, you have already done something forbidden by arranging to be alone with her.

Hashem will bring you the opportunity. He says זה או ענין כיוצא בו, this or something similar. If that woman was hit by a bus, there could be another woman you find similarly attractive. You don't have to take life insurance out on any woman you've had an affair with.

You can if you want. I'm not telling you what to do.

Anyway.

Hashem will not bring you this test unless He knows you will not fail again. So if He brings you back into the situation where you failed, it is a sign that you have been repaired and your teshuva was accepted.

## A lost object in Paris

Rav Tzadok brings the Gemara in {{< tooltip term="Sanhedrin" key="sanhedrin-tractate" def="The tractate of the Talmud on courts, capital cases and punishments." >}} about a butcher caught selling nonkosher meat.

Now he wants to do teshuva. Of course he is not selling {{< tooltip term="treif" key="treif" >}} anymore. People already suspect him; the jig is up. That could just be self-preservation. So the Gemara says he has to go somewhere nobody knows him and prove himself there: return a valuable lost object, or throw out valuable treif meat of his own at a loss.[^butcher]

The second option will come to him in the ordinary course of things. He is a butcher; sooner or later an expensive animal of his will turn out to be treif, and he can declare it so and take the loss. Rav Tzadok's question is only about the first. You cannot decide where you will find a lost object. You find it where you find it.

> והרי זה אין בידם אם אין מוצאין אבידה מה יעשו.
>
> This is not in their hands. If they do not find a lost object, what are they supposed to do?

What, now he can't do teshuva?

If you were just at home, fine, people find lost objects. But you have to go off to Paris and find one there? Who says you are going to find a lost object in Paris? Come on.

Rav Tzadok says that if your teshuva is complete, Hashem will arrange it. You will find the lost object in Paris. Don't worry about it.

Don't pick it up, though. It could be a bomb.

“Exactly,” my father said.

## Gechazi

{{< tooltip term="Gechazi" key="gechazi" >}} was Elisha's student who was not so kosher. Rav Tzadok says his sin was שחמד למה שאינו שלו: he wanted what wasn't his. I brought up his conduct toward the {{< tooltip term="Shunammite woman" key="shunammite" def="The woman of Shunem who hosted Elisha and whose son he brought back to life (2 Kings 4)." >}}. More generally, he wanted other people's things. Money, women. You shall not covet.[^gechazi]

Hashem brought him the beginning of his repair through the spoils of {{< tooltip term="Aram" key="aram" def="The Aramean kingdom centered on Damascus, Israel's northern rival." >}}. The Sages identify the lepers in that story as Gechazi and his sons. They did help themselves to some of it. But they could have kept quiet and taken more, and instead they went to tell the others. They were afraid of sin: ומצאנו עוון, “punishment will overtake us.” The man whose sin was coveting stood in front of an abandoned camp full of loot, and this time he didn't keep it to himself. Rav Tzadok calls that the beginning; Gechazi's name is not yet mentioned explicitly.

Then comes the story of the woman getting her property back through him. Gechazi did not arrange it; it came about through him on its own. That is the same principle as before. You don't go looking for the test. It finds you. Rav Tzadok says it is a sign of the completion of his repair. She got her things back through him, instead of his wanting to keep them for himself.

Afterward, Elisha went to Aram. The Sages say he went to bring Gechazi back to repentance. Elisha had kicked him out; now he went to bring him back.

Rav Tzadok connects this with the {{< tooltip term="Mishnah" key="mishnah" >}} in {{< tooltip term="Middot" key="middot-tractate" def="The tractate of the Mishnah describing the layout and measurements of the Temple." >}}, where people say to someone who has been placed under a ban:

> השוכן בבית הזה יתן בלבם ויקרבוך.
>
> May the One who dwells in this House put it in their hearts to bring you near.

Even after all this repair, Gechazi still needed to be brought close to Elisha. And he merited that the person who had pushed him away was himself the one who brought him close again.

But the Sages list Gechazi among the people who have no share in the World to Come. They also say that he did not want to come back. Rav Tzadok says that, nevertheless, {{< tooltip term="דורשי רשומות" key="dorshei-reshumot" def="“Expounders of hints”: sages who read hidden allusions in the verses." >}} said he does have a share.

“What does he mean by that?” my father asked. “Just the literal meaning of what you just said.”

There is a contradiction about whether Gechazi gets the World to Come. In one place, he is explicitly on the list of people who do not. But there is also the reading we had [last time](/post/weakness-is-greatness/), finding hints in Psalms that various people do have a share. Gechazi is one of them.

So explicitly, Gechazi does not get the World to Come. In this hidden way, through that reading, he does. I am not a hundred percent sure what that means. Presumably, in Rav Tzadok's world, there is some revealed way in which he doesn't and some hidden way in which he does. And if we are going Rav Tzadok all the way, the hidden way is higher than the revealed way, and so on.

## Take two

My father thought this returned to what we had been learning: teshuva on teshuva, descent before ascent, and the thing that made something nonkosher being the means by which you {{< tooltip term="kasher" key="kashering" >}} it.

Yes. We said [last time](/post/weakness-is-greatness/) that your weakness is your greatness. Now he brings תשובת המשקל: where you failed is where you have your repair. You have to be in the situation of the failure to repair it. Once my father pointed that out, it seemed clear. He was right.

And Rav Tzadok stretches the term to get there. תשובת המשקל usually means penance weighed against the sin: suffering in proportion to the pleasure you took, so the scales balance.[^mishkal] Rav Tzadok moves the weight. What balances the sin is not a matching amount of pain but the matching situation: the same woman, the same time, the same place, and this time you walk away.

I wanted to add something I probably should have said earlier. Usually, when people read that Rambam, they do not take it to mean that this has to happen. If you have the same opportunity and refrain, that is complete teshuva. You failed; take two, you did it right. But you do not necessarily read it as a promise that you will get take two.

There is also the Rambam's language about the One who knows what is hidden testifying that you will not return to the sin. Rav Tzadok says that when Hashem knows you will not fail, He gives you the opportunity to show it.[^rambam]

I was not entirely sure whether he meant that it has to happen. But he is saying that when it does happen, it is a sign that your teshuva is complete.

Why does Hashem need to do that? Maybe it is for you. After the sin, perhaps you need to realize that you are not the man you used to be. Or something like that.

By the time the test comes, the change has already happened. Hashem knows you would not do it again. He says here that Hashem is not going to give you the test unless He knows you will not fail.

You can go and seek out the test yourself. If you want to do it, you'll find a way. But if you are not looking for it, Hashem brings it to you to show you. That's how He shows you that you did complete teshuva.
