I could read the two-word inscription on an axe. The letter was considerably harder. And the poetry was a case where I probably should have understood more than I did, but apparently I wasn't prepared for it to rain oil.

That was my latest video, in an ongoing experiment of trying to read ancient {{< tooltip term="Semitic languages" key="semitic-languages" >}} using the languages I already know. This time I tried {{< tooltip term="Ugaritic" key="ugaritic" >}}.

{{< youtube mD0hhGj7W6E >}}

My background, for anyone coming into this series here, is Hebrew, the {{< tooltip term="Aramaic" key="aramaic" >}} you pick up from learning {{< tooltip term="Gemara" key="gemara" >}} and other Jewish texts, and some conversational Arabic. I can read {{< tooltip term="Biblical" key="biblical-hebrew" >}} and {{< tooltip term="Mishnaic Hebrew" key="mishnaic-hebrew" >}} comfortably. My Arabic is considerably less comfortable, but I can have a conversation with someone who is patient.

{{< tooltip term="Ugarit" key="ugarit" >}} was a trading kingdom on the coast of what is now {{< tooltip term="Syria" key="syria" >}}, near {{< tooltip term="Latakia" key="latakia" >}}. During the {{< tooltip term="Late Bronze Age" key="late-bronze-age" >}} it was under {{< tooltip term="Hittite" key="hittite" >}} domination. Its archives contain letters, administrative records, and literary texts, including the {{< tooltip term="Baal Cycle" key="baal-cycle" >}}.[^ugarit]

Ugaritic is a {{< tooltip term="Northwest Semitic" key="northwest-semitic" >}} language, related to Hebrew and Aramaic. Unlike {{< tooltip term="Moabite" key="moabite" >}} and {{< tooltip term="Phoenician" key="phoenician" >}}, it isn't usually classified as a {{< tooltip term="Canaanite language" key="canaanite-languages" >}}. So I expected some familiarity, but not necessarily the experience of reading slightly unusual Hebrew.

One other thing: Ugaritic uses an alphabet written in {{< tooltip term="cuneiform" key="cuneiform" >}}, from left to right. I can make my way through some of the signs, but it doesn't come naturally to me, and that isn't really what I'm testing here. For this experiment I mostly read the Ugaritic transcribed into Hebrew letters. The Hebrew and Arabic columns below are transcriptions, not translations.[^script]

## The axe

We started with a bronze axe found in the {{< tooltip term="House of the High Priest" key="house-of-the-high-priest" >}}, catalogued as {{< tooltip term="RS 1.051" key="rs-number" >}} / {{< tooltip term="KTU 6.6" key="ktu" >}}. It has two words on it:[^texts]

{{< image src="images/axe-rs-1-051.jpg" alt="Black-and-white photograph of a bronze axe head with a round socket at one end and a short line of wedge-shaped Ugaritic letters incised along the blade" caption="The inscribed axe, RS 1.051 / KTU 6.6. (Photo: Marguerite Yon, *La cité d’Ougarit sur le tell de Ras Shamra*, 1998, ill. 63, via [Mnamon, Scuola Normale Superiore](https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=30&lang=en&page=Esempi).)" >}}

| Ugaritic  | Hebrew letters | Arabic letters | Latin letters | Meaning              |
| --------- | -------------- | -------------- | ------------- | -------------------- |
| 𐎗𐎁 𐎟 𐎋𐎅𐎐𐎎 | רב כהנמ        | رب كهنم        | rb khnm       | Chief of the priests |

*{{< tooltip term="Rav kohanim" key="rav-kohanim" >}}.* Okay. That one wasn't particularly difficult.

I wouldn't necessarily have known what to do with the axe, but I could read the inscription.

## A letter to the king

The next text, KTU 2.38, was a letter from the king of {{< tooltip term="Tyre" key="tyre" >}} to the king of Ugarit. I didn't know what it said before attempting it.

I could identify a king, a brother, some sort of greeting, {{< tooltip term="Egypt" key="egypt" >}}, and rain. So there were things to work with. Unfortunately, there were also quite a few things I didn't understand, and I did my best to fill those in. Sometimes rather creatively.

For example, I saw **{{< tooltip term="רגמ" key="rgm" >}}**, which reminded me of the Hebrew root for stoning. Maybe the king's name was Rock?

After my experience with {{< tooltip term="Sabaic" key="sabaic" >}}, where proper names had caused me trouble, I was apparently prepared to solve this problem by making more things proper names.

It turns out **rgm** here means “say.” There was no King Rock. That was a contribution of my own.

{{< image src="images/letter-rs-18-031.jpg" alt="Front and back of a small clay tablet, shown one above the other, both faces covered in lines of Ugaritic cuneiform" caption="The letter from the king of Tyre, RS 18.031 / KTU 2.38, front and back. (Photo: Pierre Bordreuil and Dennis Pardee, *A Manual of Ugaritic*, 2009, text 26, via [Mnamon, Scuola Normale Superiore](https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=30&lang=en&page=Esempi).)" >}}

These are a few of the phrases from the letter, rather than a continuous passage:[^texts]

| Ugaritic                   | Hebrew letters      | Arabic letters     | Latin letters        | Meaning                                 |
| -------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------ | -------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| 𐎍 𐎟 𐎎𐎍𐎋 𐎟 𐎜𐎂𐎗𐎚 𐎟 𐎀𐎃𐎊 𐎟 𐎗𐎂𐎎 | ל מלכ אגרת אח׳י רגמ | ل ملك أجرت أخي رجم | l mlk ʾugrt ʾaḫy rgm | To the king of Ugarit, my brother, say: |
| 𐎂𐎌𐎎 𐎟 𐎀𐎄𐎗                  | גשמ אדר             | جشم أدر            | gšm ʾadr             | Mighty rain / a severe storm            |
| 𐎁 𐎟 𐎍𐎁𐎅 𐎟 𐎀𐎍 𐎟 𐎊𐎌𐎚         | ב לבה אל ישת        | ب لبه أل يشت       | b lbh ʾal yšt        | Let him not put it in his heart         |

**{{< tooltip term="גשמ אדר" key="gshm-adr" >}}** looked to me like rain in the month of {{< tooltip term="Adar" key="adar" >}}. Which seemed reasonable. It rains in Adar.

But it was *{{< tooltip term="geshem adir" key="geshem-adir" >}}*: mighty rain. Once I saw that the letter concerned ships caught in a storm near Tyre, that made considerably more sense.

I also missed **{{< tooltip term="ʾilm" key="ilm" >}}**, “the gods.” In retrospect, that was one I should have recognized. And I recognized **{{< tooltip term="מצרמ" key="mtsrm" >}}** as Egypt, but without understanding the words around it, I ended up with someone going to Egypt rather than ships being sent there.

The closing reassurance, though, I more or less got: don't put it in your heart. Don't worry about it.

So I understood that someone was telling someone else not to worry. I just hadn't understood what he wasn't supposed to worry about.

## The Baal Cycle

Then I tried two lines from the Baal Cycle:[^poetry]

{{< image src="images/baal-tablet-vi-ao-16636.jpg" alt="A large broken clay tablet on a dark background, its surface divided into ruled columns of dense Ugaritic cuneiform, with the upper right portion missing" caption="The Baal Cycle tablet KTU 1.6, Louvre AO 16636. (Photo: Shonagon, [CC0](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tablette_avec_po%C3%A8me_mythologique_du_cycle_de_Ba%27al_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Antiquit%C3%A9s_orientales_AO_16636.jpg).)" >}}

| Ugaritic          | Hebrew letters | Arabic letters | Latin letters | Meaning                       |
| ----------------- | -------------- | -------------- | ------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 𐎌𐎎𐎎 𐎟 𐎌𐎎𐎐 𐎟 𐎚𐎎𐎉𐎗𐎐 | שממ שמנ תמטרנ  | شمم شمن تمطرن  | šmm šmn tmṭrn | May the heavens rain oil      |
| 𐎐𐎃𐎍𐎎 𐎟 𐎚𐎍𐎋 𐎟 𐎐𐎁𐎚𐎎 | נח׳למ תלכ נבתמ | نخلم تلك نبتم  | nḫlm tlk nbtm | May the wadis flow with honey |

I got something about the heavens raining, and something about streams going somewhere. I didn't know the word for honey. But the oil was sitting right there: **{{< tooltip term="שמנ" key="shemen" >}}**. *Shemen.*

I think the problem was that I wasn't expecting the heavens to rain oil. So instead of accepting a reading that was fairly close to Hebrew, I assumed I was missing something.

As I said in the video, not all of it is a language issue. Sometimes it's just a skill issue.

In this case, the sentence actually made the individual words harder for me to recognize. Usually context helps. Here I might have done better if someone had handed me each word separately, because I wouldn't have been trying to make the weather report sensible.

## How close did it feel?

I couldn't read Ugaritic straight through with anything like the ease of reading a passage from the {{< tooltip term="Torah" key="torah" >}}. Moabite and Phoenician had felt much closer to that. With Ugaritic, I kept recognizing words and then losing the sentence.

But once I looked at the translations, a lot of it felt accessible. Some words were simply unfamiliar. I didn't know the word for honey, and staring at it longer wasn't going to help. Others I knew perfectly well. **שמנ** is *shemen*. I didn't miss it because it was foreign. I missed it because heavens raining oil didn't sound like something a sentence would say, so I went looking for a reading that made more sense. Those aren't the same problem, even if they look the same on camera.

With Sabaic, I often felt that I simply didn't have enough to go on. Here I felt that knowing a few more words would have made a substantial difference. I wouldn't claim that I understood the letter, because I didn't. But I could see how I might get there.

And the Baal passage was closer than my first attempt suggested. Apparently I needed to be a little more open-minded about what could fall out of the sky.
